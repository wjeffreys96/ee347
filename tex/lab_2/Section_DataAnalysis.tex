\paragraph{}Something else worth mentioning is how the real power and voltage stayed relatively
constant throughout both tests. The line voltage remained around 208 V, while the real power stayed
between approximately 72 and 74 W. This makes sense because changing the capacitive load shouldn't
have much of an effect on the real power being consumed by the original load. Instead, the capacitors
affect the reactive power being supplied by the source. This helps explain why we saw changes in line
current even though the real power stayed nearly the same. As the power factor gets closer to 1, the
apparent power decreases, which also reduces the line current.

\paragraph{}Looking at the reactive power measurements helps us better understand what was happening
with the power factor. For the purely resistive load, we started with almost no reactive power. However,
after adding the -j171 Ω capacitor bank, the reactive power dropped to -253.1 VAR. This shows that the
circuit was now supplying reactive power back toward the source. As we increased the magnitude of capacitive
reactance, the reactive power started moving closer to zero. This is what we would expect because increasing
capacitive reactance means we are actually decreasing capacitance, resulting in less reactive power being supplied.

\paragraph{}For the inductive load, we saw something different. Without any capacitors connected, the circuit
was consuming 66.65 VAR. By adding capacitors in parallel, we were able to offset some of that inductive reactive
power. At -j600 Ω, the reactive power had dropped to just -6.779 VAR, showing that the inductive and capacitive
reactive powers were nearly canceling each other out. This was also where we saw the power factor reach almost
1 and the line current drop to one of its lowest values.

\paragraph{}Something interesting about the inductive circuit was how adding too much capacitance actually made
the power factor worse. When we first connected the -j171 Ω capacitor bank, we ended up supplying more capacitive
reactive power than was needed to offset the inductive load. This caused the line current to increase from 0.276 A
to 0.621 A, while the calculated power factor dropped from 0.74 to 0.37. So instead of improving the power factor,
we actually made it worse by overcorrecting the circuit.

\paragraph{}As we increased the magnitude of capacitive reactance, the circuit started moving closer to the ideal
correction point at -j600 Ω where the parallel combination completely cancels out reactance. Here, the line current had decreased to 0.224 A, which is about a 19\% reduction
compared to the original inductive load without any capacitors. However, when we increased the capacitive
reactance to -j1200 Ω, the circuit became inductive again because we were no longer supplying enough capacitive
reactive power to fully offset the load. This shows why it's important to use the correct amount of capacitance
when correcting power factor. Too much or too little can move the power factor away from 1, increasing the line
current instead of reducing it.

\paragraph{}It's necessary to point out one row of bad data in table 1. When looking at
the rows for inductive supply offset by the capacitive loads of -j240 and -j400 the
power factor angles are identical. Which indicates that while we were switching between
capacitive loads we switched incorrectly and recorded the -j240 offset twice. This doesn't effect
the rest of our measurements however and the pattens we see still tell us a lot about the system.

\paragraph{}Another discrepancy we need to discuss is our measured power factor and power factor angle.
If you look at the puerly resistive load with no offset on the first row of table 1 you will see a
phase angle of 30$^\circ$. This is clearly different than the expected 0 degrees. The reason for this is
that while we were setting up the computer to take measurement we told it to use $V_{LL}$ as the reference voltage instead
of $V_\phi$. The reason this causes a particular offset of 30$^\circ$ arises because line voltage and phase
voltage are related by $V_{LL}=\sqrt{3}V_{\phi}\angle30^\circ$. Because of this unintended offset we
also calculated proper values for our phase angle and power factor and included them in table 1. Table 2
shows the ideal or theoretical values for each measurement and was used to compare our results with what
was expected.

\paragraph{}The figures below consist of two kinds of measurements. One being a phasor analysis
showing our AC voltage and current in the complex domain in terms of phase angles. The other being
an oscilloscope, which is showing the current and voltage in the familiar time domain. Both
graphs show the phase angle between voltage and current, and when looking through them we can
see how our different capacitive loads increase or decrease the angle between them.

\paragraph{}If you look at figures 1 and 2, which show the voltage and current from the purely
resistive load with a -j171$\Omega$ offset, we can observe a phase angle of around 74$^\circ$
which closely aligns with what we see in table 1. The following two figures, 3 and 4,
are from the same initial load. This time with an offset of -j600$\Omega$. From them we
can see how the phase angle has decreased to about 45$^\circ$. Then if we move our
attention all the way to tables 5 and 6 we can see the smallest phase angle since adding
the various capacitive loads of only 26.6$^\circ$.

\paragraph{}Aside from the changing phase angles the 6 graphs also show us how the amplitude
of the current is changing with the different loads. Specifically it shows how it decreases
with larger capacitive loads. This can be seen in more detail in table 1. Something surprising
from this is how the amplitude of current initially increases after adding a -j170 parallel
load. But then starts decreasing as more capacitance is added. This is the inverse of what
happened to the power factor, which initially drops down to 0.20 followed by a steady incline
as the capacitance increases.

\paragraph{}The bottom 6 graphs show the same sequence of secondary capacitive loads this
time for the initial inductive impedance of 300+j300$\Omega$. The pattern closely resembles
the previous test case. Adding capacitive loads starts decreasing current and increasing the
power factor as we offset some of the inductive load. At $X_C=600\Omega$ the parallel
impedances cancel each other out and we reach a power factor of 1 again. When $X_C=1200\Omega$
we get an overcorrection and the power factor angle moves towards -26$^\circ$.
