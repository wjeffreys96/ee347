\paragraph{}The most noticeable thing on the table of measurements is likely how far
the power factors are from the theoretical values. This is easy to see just from the
first data point in table 1, which shows how a purely resistive load of 600$\Omega$
has a measured power factor of 0.866 instead of 1.... Right? ...

\paragraph{}It's necessary to mention one row of bad data in table 1. When looking at
the rows for inductive supply offset by the capacitive loads of -j240 and -j400 the
power factor angles are identical. Which indicates that while we were switching between
capacitive loads we switched incorrectly and recorded the -j240 offset twice.

\paragraph{}The figures bellow consist of two kinds of measurements. One being a phasor analysis
showing our AC voltage and current in the complex domain in terms of phase angles. The other being
an oscilloscope, which is showing the current and voltage in the familiar time domain. Both
graphs show the phase angle between voltage and current, and when looking through them we can
see how our different capacitive loads increase or decrease the angle between them.

\paragraph{}If you look at figures 1 and 2, which show the voltage and current from the purely
resistive load with a -j171$\Omega$ offset, we can observe a phase angle of around 74$^\circ$
which closely aligns with what we see in table 1. The following two figures, 3 and 4,
are from the same initial load. This time with an offset of -j600$\Omega$. From them we
can see how the phase angle has decreased to about 45$^\circ$. Then if we move our
attention all the way to tables 5 and 6 we can see the smallest phase angle since adding
the various capacitive loads of only 26.6$^\circ$.

\paragraph{}Aside from the changing phase angles the 6 graphs also show us how the amplitude
of the current is changing with the different loads. Specifically it shows how it decreases
with larger capacitive loads. This can be seen in more detail in table 1. Something surprising
from this is how the amplitude of current initially increases after adding a -j170 parallel
load. But then starts decreasing as more capacitance is added. This is the inverse of what
happened to the power factor, which initially drops down to 0.20 followed by a steady incline
as the capacitance increases.

\paragraph{}The bottom 6 graphs show the same sequence of secondary capacitive loads this
time for the initial inductive impedance of 300+j300$\Omega$. The pattern closely resembles
the previous test case. Adding capacitive loads starts decreasing current and increasing the
power factor as we offset some of the inductive load. At $X_C=600\Omega$ the parallel
impedances cancel each other out and we reach a power factor of 1 again. When $X_C=1200\Omega$
we get an overcorrection and the power factor angle moves towards -26$^\circ$.
